01 A material that sorts light by its quantum statistics

Quantum Statistical Plasmonic Metacrystals

Co-first author·Quantum Photonics Group, Louisiana State University · Baton Rouge, LA, USA·Nature 655, 885–891 (2026)

Techniques
Photon-number-resolving detection · quantum-state characterization · confocal microscopy
Modeling
ANSYS Lumerical FDTD electromagnetic simulations of the nanoantennas
Recognition
Nature News & Views highlight · covered by Phys.org, ScienceDaily and LSU News

The question

Metasurfaces can make large, complex optical systems more compact by building light control into the material. But most metasurfaces control wavelength, frequency, or spatial mode—not the quantum statistics of multiphoton light.

In solar cells, scattering can trap light before it reaches the active layer, while added noise used to break that localization can cost energy. Because the quantum statistics of sunlight can affect how it propagates through a material, we asked: can we control those statistics to break localization without needing extra energy?

Approach

  • Designed metacrystals made of gold nanoantennas (“meta-atoms”) that couple light into surface plasmons, modeling their optical response with Lumerical FDTD.
  • Sent multiphoton light with different statistics through the structures and measured the output with photon-number-resolving detectors and correlation measurements.
  • Imaged the plasmonic fields and samples with confocal microscopy to connect geometry to the measured statistics.

Key results

  • The metacrystals show allowed and forbidden “statistical bands”: light whose statistics fall in an allowed band passes undistorted, while light in a forbidden band is suppressed or pushed to the nearest allowed state.
  • The bands are set by the size, number and orientation of the meta-atoms, giving a deterministic way to engineer how quantum light is transported.
  • Works at room temperature, pointing to coherence-sensitive photonic materials for scalable quantum technologies and energy harvesting.

My contribution

Co-first author. I helped design and carry out the experiments, ran the FDTD modeling of the plasmonic structures, implemented the photon-number-resolving measurements and confocal microscopy, and analyzed the data with the team. I also contributed to developing the original idea.

02 Detecting hydrogen faster with quantum light

Scalable Quantum Sensing with Robust Multiparticle Plasmonic Systems

First author·Physics and Chemical Engineering, Louisiana State University · Baton Rouge, LA, USA·Nature Communications, accepted in principle (2026)

Platform
Gold/palladium plasmonic nanoslit sensor in a gas cell, read out with photon-number-resolving detectors
Tools
LabVIEW-synchronized acquisition · hydrogen calibration · Lumerical FDTD · Fisher-information analysis
Result
~100× faster detection and ~46× lower detection limit than the classical readout

The question

Quantum-enhanced plasmonic sensing could improve measurements in biosensing and chemical detection, but many approaches rely on squeezed or entangled light sources that are complex and costly to generate. Could classical thermal light be transformed into a multiphoton state that behaves quantum mechanically, bringing quantum-sensing benefits to a simpler setup? We test this idea with hydrogen, where leaks must be detected quickly and at low concentrations.

Approach

  • Built a sensor in which multiparticle plasmons in a multimode nanoslit probe a palladium film; hydrogen turns palladium into palladium hydride and slightly reshapes the plasmonic modes.
  • Read out the light with photon-number-resolving detectors and extracted multiparticle subsystems of up to thirty particles from the measured photon statistics.
  • Developed the hydrogen calibration procedure and LabVIEW data acquisition, and used Fisher information to compare quantum and classical readouts taken from the same data.

Key results

  • Quantum enhancement that grows exponentially with the number of extracted particles, even under substantial noise and loss.
  • ~100× faster hydrogen detection (more than two orders of magnitude) and a ~46× lower detection limit than the classical protocol.
  • The protocol is general and can be applied to other plasmonic sensing platforms.

My contribution

First author. I performed the simulations, built the optical setup, ran the experiments, analyzed the data, and contributed to sample fabrication and to writing the paper, working with collaborators in LSU’s Department of Chemical Engineering on the gold and palladium films.

03 Quantum coherence that appears as light travels

Emergence of Multiphoton Quantum Coherence by Light Propagation

First author·Quantum Photonics Group, Louisiana State University · Baton Rouge, LA, USA·ACS Photonics 11, 3197–3204 (2024)

Techniques
Polarization quantum-state tomography · SLM polarization gratings · SNSPD photon-number resolution
Analysis
Python density-matrix reconstruction · MATLAB · Mathematica
Presentation
LAOP 2024 talk

The question

The quantum character of light is usually thought to be fixed by its source, and to change only when light interacts with matter. Can the quantum coherence of a multiphoton field change simply as it travels through free space?

Approach

  • Scattered thermal multiphoton light off a polarization grating displayed on a spatial light modulator.
  • Used a tunable telescope to select different propagation planes and performed polarization tomography with superconducting nanowire photon-number-resolving detectors.
  • Reconstructed density matrices in Python and compared with theory developed in MATLAB and Mathematica.

Key results

  • The quantum coherence of the multiphoton field changes upon free-space propagation, without any light–matter interaction.
  • Scattering can produce multiphoton subsystems with sub-shot-noise properties from thermal light.
  • The observations are explained by a nonclassical version of the van Cittert–Zernike theorem.

My contribution

First author. I led the experiment, performed the polarization tomography and photon-number-resolving measurements, reconstructed the density matrices, and carried out the theoretical analysis with the team.

04 Separating classical and quantum coherence

Isolating the Classical and Quantum Coherence of a Multiphoton System

Co-author, PhotoniX 5, 39 (2024)·Quantum Photonics Group, Louisiana State University · Baton Rouge, LA, USA

Light source
Pseudothermal light with classical statistics
System size
Isolated multiphoton subsystems of up to 40 photons
Methods
Orbital angular momentum (OAM) projections · photon-number-resolving detection · correlation measurements

The question

Can a classical pseudothermal light field contain isolated multiphoton subsystems with their own quantum coherence and dynamics?

Approach

  • Generated a pseudothermal optical field and projected it into selected OAM modes using angular double slits and spatial light modulators.
  • Used photon-number-resolving measurements and correlation analysis to isolate and characterize multiphoton subsystems.

Key results

  • Isolated multiphoton subsystems with up to 40 photons from a classical thermal field.
  • Found that while most subsystems exhibit classical coherence, some show quantum coherence and distinct multiparticle interference patterns.
  • Showed how OAM correlations and the coherence of each subsystem shape its observed dynamics.

My contribution

Co-author. I contributed to the experimental study of the isolated multiphoton subsystems and their classical and quantum coherence properties.

05 Computing with many photons at room temperature

Multiphoton Quantum Reservoirs for Robust Multidimensional Computing

Co-author, Advanced Science (2026)·Published research·Quantum Photonics Group, Louisiana State University · Baton Rouge, LA, USA

Degrees of freedom
Photon number · orbital angular momentum (OAM) · polarization
Hardware
Spatial light modulators · q-plates · APD-based photon-number resolution with a time tagger
Scale
Up to forty-particle systems in a reservoir of 861 components

The question

Quantum information processing usually needs fragile quantum light sources and very low noise. Reservoir computing instead uses a complex, fixed network and trains only the readout. Can a multiphoton optical reservoir built from ordinary classical light still show quantum advantages at room temperature, despite noise and loss?

Approach

  • Inject classical light into a five-step photonic circuit of half-wave plates, q-plates and quarter-wave plates that couples polarization and OAM.
  • Measure the output in both the OAM basis (with SLMs) and the photon-number basis (with APDs and a time tagger), and use projective measurements to extract multiphoton quantum subsystems from the classical field.

Key results

  • Quantum random walks that stay ballistic despite noise, recovered by projecting onto higher photon-number components.
  • Simulations of bosonic and fermionic many-body dynamics and of thermalization in synthetic lattices, plus quantum prediction of mathematical functions.

My contribution

Co-author on the published Advanced Science study of multiphoton quantum reservoirs for robust, multidimensional information processing.

06 Quantum imaging with natural light

Multiphoton Quantum Imaging Using Natural Light

Co-author·Quantum Photonics Group, Louisiana State University · Baton Rouge, LA, USA·Applied Physics Reviews 12, 011424 (2025)

Camera
Single-pixel camera with a digital micromirror device (DMD) and two PNR detectors
Methods
Compressive sensing · conditional photon-number detection · raster scanning
Light source
Thermal (“natural”) light, no fragile quantum source needed

The question

Quantum imaging can beat classical limits, but it usually relies on dim, fragile sources of quantum light that are easily swamped by background noise. Can ordinary thermal light be used instead?

Approach & results

  • Light scattered by an object is projected onto random binary patterns on a DMD and counted with photon-number-resolving detectors.
  • Conditioning on photon number isolates multiphoton subsystems and gives an exponential enhancement in image contrast.
  • The scheme recovers images of objects that are completely hidden by noise in the classical image.

My contribution

I contributed to the experimental development and data collection, including raster-scanning measurements for high-contrast image reconstruction under strong background noise.

07 Robust states in a lossy acoustic waveguide

Observation of Robust Zero-Energy Extended States

First author, Physical Review Applied 19, L061002 (2023)·University of Texas Rio Grande Valley · Edinburg, TX, USA

System
Lossy acoustic waveguide with 17 coupled resonators
Tools
COMSOL Multiphysics · 3D printing · microphones and network analyzer in an anechoic chamber
Measurement
Transmission response under controlled structural disorder

The question

Can a carefully engineered lossy acoustic system host an extended bulk state that remains stable even when the structure is imperfect?

Approach

  • Modeled and built a 3D-printed acoustic waveguide made from 17 coupled resonators.
  • Introduced controlled disorder and measured the transmission response, comparing the observations with COMSOL simulations.

Key results

  • Observed an extended bulk state near 257 Hz whose frequency shifted by only ~0.35 Hz under 10% random disorder.
  • Other resonances shifted by about 1.5 Hz under the same disorder, making this state more than 4× more robust.

My contribution

First author. I carried out the theoretical and experimental work, including COMSOL modeling, waveguide characterization, controlled-disorder experiments and transmission analysis.

08 Filtering sound with engineered loss

Tunable Non-Hermitian Acoustic Filter

Co-first author, Physical Review Applied 16, 014012 (2021)·University of Texas Rio Grande Valley · Edinburg, TX, USA

Structure
3D-printed superlattice of coupled hollow cuboid resonators
Tools
COMSOL Multiphysics · 3D printing · microphones and network analyzer
Control
Engineered loss in one sublattice tunes absorption and reflection by frequency

The question

Can engineered loss turn an acoustic waveguide into a tunable filter that selectively absorbs or reflects chosen frequencies?

Approach & results

  • Designed and 3D-printed a non-Hermitian acoustic superlattice of hollow cuboids.
  • Adding loss to one sublattice creates absorption resonances, allowing selected frequencies to be absorbed or reflected.
  • Experimental measurements agreed with COMSOL simulations.

My contribution

Co-first author. I collected the experimental data and verified the results against COMSOL simulations.

09 M.S. thesis in mathematical physics

Solving Nonlinear Schrödinger Equations Using the Darboux Transformation Method

Master of Science thesis·University of Dhaka · Dhaka, Bangladesh·March 2016

Focus
Nonlinear Schrödinger equations
Methods
Darboux transformations · Lax pairs
Computational tool
Mathematica

The question

How can Darboux transformation methods be used to construct analytical solutions to nonlinear Schrödinger equations?

Approach

  • Studied Darboux transformations and their relation to Lax-pair formulations.
  • Applied the method to construct solitonic solutions, including sech-shaped and Peregrine-type solutions.
  • Used Mathematica for symbolic calculations and solution analysis.

My contribution
